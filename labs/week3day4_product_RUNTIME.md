## Where we're heading

The app and the context layer run on AWS Lambda behind an AgentCore Gateway, and the claim agent works through the Gateway's tools, but the agent's service still runs on a laptop beside the app. This change makes the agent an AgentCore app, which runs the same way on a laptop and on AgentCore Runtime. On Runtime each dispatch or evaluation runs in a session of its own. It also brings back a handler's review of a large approval: a senior handler signs it off, as now, or sends it back to the agent with a note, and the agent decides again, in a new session when it's on Runtime.

Our platform team has added two folders for the deployment. `agentcore/` is the AgentCore project, which deploys the agent with the AgentCore CLI, and `runtime/main.py` is the file Runtime starts, which runs the agent's AgentCore app from `agent/entrypoint.py` (step 3). Treat both as theirs: change the product to fit them rather than changing them, and if something in them stops the agent working, say so in the plan.

Each step below says what has to be true when it's finished and how we'll check it. The requirements describe what the product does rather than how its code is organized, and the notes at the end say where things are in our last build.

## What we found

- An approval over the approval limit ends the claim agent's work. The claim waits for a senior handler's sign-off, which pays it, but a handler who thinks the payout is wrong has no way to tell the agent, and can only leave the claim waiting.
- The agent's service is a FastAPI app with a route for each kind of work, which starts the work with FastAPI's `BackgroundTasks` after it replies. On Runtime an agent answers on port 8080 at `/invocations`, which takes a JSON payload, and at `/ping`, which tells Runtime whether it's still busy. An AgentCore app, `BedrockAgentCoreApp` from the `bedrock-agentcore` package, serves both. Runtime ends a session once it has been idle for the runtime's idle timeout, a minute in our project, even if work is still going on a thread the AgentCore app doesn't know about.
- The agent's service keeps one dispatch, evaluation or round at a time in its own memory. It marks every running run stopped when it starts, and every running run or open round stopped when its work on one fails. On Runtime each dispatch has a session of its own, so two sessions can be working at once, and one session starting or failing would stop the other's work.
- The app reaches the agent's service at an address, `UNDERCOVER_AGENT`, with no key. That's fine on a laptop, where the service only listens on 127.0.0.1, but not on a host of its own.
- The agent's checkpoint pool prepares its statements (`prepare_threshold=0`). On Runtime the agent reaches Supabase through the transaction pooler on port 6543, as the app on Lambda does, and the transaction pooler can't keep prepared statements: in our experiment, 6 of 30 queries failed with `DuplicatePreparedStatement`.

## Step 1: The claim approval comes back

1. When the claim agent records an approval that waits for sign-off, a payout over the approval limit or a payment the Gateway refused, the claim graph pauses with LangGraph's `interrupt()`, with its place saved on the claim's thread in Supabase. The claim waits for sign-off as it does today, and a run counts it as handled as it does today. A claim is paused when its thread has an interrupt waiting for an answer.
2. An evaluation's copy doesn't pause, since nobody signs off a copy: its work ends as it does today.
3. The graph resumes with a handler's answer: signed off, or sent back with a note (step 2). After a sign-off, the claim agent's work ends as it does for any decision, and the claim is saved to memory when memory is on. The payment stays the app's: the agent never pays over the limit.
4. The agent gets an operation, `resume_claim`, that resumes a paused claim with the handler's answer. The request names only the claim, and the agent reads the answer from the app's record: paid with a sign-off, or sent back with a note. It answers at once that it has taken the claim, as the agent's other operations do, and works the claim in the background, outside any run, on the claim agents of the current version. It refuses a claim that isn't paused, or a sent-back claim it can't take (step 2), as they refuse work, with the reason. A resume runs beside a dispatch or an evaluation and never waits for one.
5. A sign-off goes through only while the claim is waiting for sign-off, so a sign-off and a send back at the same moment can't both happen. It records who signed off and pays, as now, and then asks the agent to resume the claim. The sign-off answers with the updated claim whatever happens to that call. When the call fails or times out, the app logs a warning, the sign-off and the payment stand, and if the agent never resumed the claim it isn't saved to memory. `docs/STATUS.md` lists this among the known gaps.

Done when:

- Tests with stand-ins cover an approval over the limit that pauses with its place saved, a payment the Gateway refused that pauses, an evaluation's copy that doesn't pause, a resume after a sign-off that ends the work and saves the claim to a stand-in memory, and `resume_claim` refusing a claim that isn't paused.
- With `npm run dev` running, a claim over the limit pauses, and signing it off on the dashboard pays it and ends the agent's work.

## Step 2: Sign off or send back

1. On a claim waiting for sign-off, a handler who can sign off over the limit sees two buttons: Sign off, as now, and Send back, with a note saying what to look at again. The note is required, up to 1,000 characters.
2. The app's `send_back_claim` operation takes the note from the signed-in handler, with the same check on the handler as the sign-off, and goes through only while the claim is waiting for sign-off. It refuses an evaluation's copy, and a claim that isn't waiting for sign-off, with a 409. It keeps the decision that was sent back on the claim, with the note, who sent it back and when, clears the claim's current decision, records the note as a step on the claim, and puts the claim in a new status, sent back. It then asks the agent to resume the claim.
3. A sent-back claim is taken for work once: the agent's resume, or a dispatch, moves it from sent back to in progress through an app operation that only the agent's key can call, and only the first caller gets it. A resume then holds its claim as a run holds its work (step 4): it tells the app every minute while it works, and a dispatch leaves alone a claim in progress whose resume the app has heard from in the last five minutes. A dispatch takes sent-back claims along with the waiting ones, so a send back the agent never received, because it couldn't be reached or its session ended first, is worked by the next dispatch, and a dispatch that carries on a claim whose thread is still paused resumes it with the note. The send back answers with the updated claim whatever happens to the call to the agent, logging a warning when that call fails or times out, and the claim page says when a claim is waiting for the next dispatch.
4. Whoever takes the claim resumes its thread with the note from the app's record. The agent gives the model the note as a message from the handler, naming who sent the decision back. The graph forgets the earlier decision first, so nothing can pay it, and the claim agent has its full allowance of model calls again. It works on until it records a new decision, which goes through the same checks as any other: an approval needs its calculation, a payout over the limit waits for sign-off again, and one within the limit is paid. A claim can be sent back more than once.
5. The message carrying the note says how to treat it: as a senior handler's review of the decision, checked against the policy wording and the claims manual like everything else, with the claim referred to a handler, giving the reason, when the wording doesn't support what the note asks. It's part of the agent's code rather than its instructions, so a new version of the instructions from the improver can't drop it. `backend/instructions/claim.md` changes only where it says a decision can't be changed.
6. The queue shows sent-back claims with their status. The claim page shows each decision that was sent back, with its note, who sent it back and when, beside the current decision, and the note among the claim's steps. Its words follow the dashboard's plain style and `docs/BRAND.md`.

Done when:

- Tests cover the send back's refusals, a sign-off and a send back at the same moment, the decision kept with its note, a sent-back claim taken once when the resume and a dispatch both ask, a dispatch started during a resume that leaves its claim alone, a new decision that's paid and one that waits for sign-off again, and a send back the agent never received that the next dispatch works.
- With `npm run dev` running, a claim sent back with a note is decided again by the agent, and the claim page shows both decisions and the note, checked through the Playwright MCP server.

## Step 3: The agent as an AgentCore app

1. The agent becomes an AgentCore app in place of its FastAPI service: `agent/entrypoint.py` makes `app = BedrockAgentCoreApp()` with one entrypoint, and the platform team's `runtime/main.py` runs it. Each payload names an operation and carries its inputs. The operations are the service's, by their operation ids, with step 1's `resume_claim`, and their inputs are the ones each route takes today: `{"operation": "start_dispatch", "agents": 25}`, for example, or `{"operation": "resume_claim", "claim_id": "CLM-1001"}`. Each answers `{"accepted": true}` when it has taken the work, or `{"refused": "<the reason>"}` where the service answers with a 409 today, such as a dispatch asked for while another is running. `{"operation": "health"}` answers `{"healthy": true}`. An operation the agent doesn't know, or a payload with an input missing or wrong, gets a 400 that says what's wrong.
2. Each operation checks what it must, answers at once, and does its work on a thread of its own. Before it answers, the agent registers the work with the AgentCore app as an async task (`app.add_async_task`), and it completes the task when the work ends, however it ends (`app.complete_async_task`). The AgentCore app's own ping then says `HealthyBusy` while any work is going and `Healthy` once it's all done, so on Runtime a session lasts as long as its work.
3. Nothing happens when the agent's code is imported or as its AgentCore app starts. On Runtime's platform version V2, every session starts from one snapshot, so a key fetched or a connection opened before the first request would be shared by every session. The agent gets ready on its first request, once in its process, before it answers. On Runtime it reseeds Python's random numbers and fetches its credentials from AgentCore Identity: the deployment gives it a setting `AGENTCORE_CREDENTIAL_<NAME>_NAME` for each credential, naming its provider in Identity, and the agent fetches the value into the setting `<NAME>`, where `.env` puts it on the laptop. Then, as the service does when it starts today, it checks it has the agent's key, opens its checkpoint pool, and marks stopped the runs and rounds left running, which step 4 takes out. If getting ready fails, the request answers with the error and the next request tries again. A health check gets ready like any other request, so it answers only once the agent has its settings and its pool.
4. Off Runtime, the agent refuses a request without the agent's key with a 401, apart from the health check, so it can run on a host of its own. The app sends the key as `Authorization: Bearer <key>` with each request it posts to the agent. On Runtime, AWS checks that the caller may invoke the runtime before a request reaches the agent, so the app sends no key and the agent asks for none. The agent knows it's on Runtime from the credentials' settings (item 3).
5. `npm run dev` serves the agent's AgentCore app with uvicorn on 127.0.0.1:8080, the port AgentCore apps use, with reload, in place of the service on 8002. The app posts each payload to `/invocations` at `UNDERCOVER_AGENT`, by default `http://127.0.0.1:8080`, with the 10-second timeout it has today.
6. When `UNDERCOVER_AGENT_RUNTIME` is set to the ARN of the agent's runtime, the app sends the same payload to that runtime instead, with boto3's `invoke_agent_runtime`, in the region the app runs in, and reads the same answer. Each request starts a new session, with an id of its own at least 33 characters long. The app makes one attempt at each call, with no retries, since a retried call could start the same work twice. It waits up to 5 seconds to connect and 20 to read, inside Lambda's 30, leaving time for its own writes.
7. On either route, the agent didn't take the work when the request was never sent or never connected, when the app got an error answer, or when the agent refused. Anything else that goes wrong once the request was sent, such as a timeout or an answer cut off, may have started the work, so the app changes nothing. Either way the handler gets the answer they get today: a refusal comes through as the service's 409 does, and a timeout tells them to reload the page to see whether it went through. The sign-off and the send back still answer with the updated claim whatever happens to the call (steps 1 and 2).
8. The agent's checkpoint pool turns prepared statements off (`prepare_threshold=None`), so it works through either of Supabase's poolers.
9. `bedrock-agentcore` joins the project's dependencies. `pyproject.toml` gets a `runtime` dependency group with exactly what the agent needs on Runtime, `bedrock-agentcore` included. `npm run check` checks that `runtime.main` imports with only that group installed, in a temporary environment of its own, as it already does for the `server` and `context` groups. The deployment installs this group at `uv.lock`'s versions.
10. `boto3` joins the `server` group, so the app's package has it.

Done when:

- Tests call the agent's AgentCore app through Starlette's `TestClient`, with stand-ins for the work and for Identity: importing the agent opens nothing, on Runtime the first request fetches each credential into its setting once, each operation accepts work and refuses it, a payload with an input missing, the ping busy while work is going and healthy once it has finished or failed, a request without the key refused off Runtime and let through on Runtime, and the health check without the key.
- Tests cover the app's two routes with stand-ins: the key on the laptop route, the session id on the Runtime route, and on both the payload, an acceptance, a refusal passed on, a failed connection, an error answer, and a timeout or a cut-off answer that changes nothing.
- The runtime group's check passes in `npm run check`.
- With `npm run dev` stopped, `uv run --env-file .env --isolated --only-group runtime python -m runtime.main` serves the agent on port 8080, and `{"operation": "health"}` posted to its `/invocations` answers `{"healthy": true}`.

## Step 4: The app keeps one run at a time

1. The app keeps one piece of the agent's work going at a time: a dispatch, an evaluation, or a round of the improver while it works, which is while the round is open with no proposal waiting for a handler's review. A handler who asks to start another while one is going gets a 409 that names what's going, as the agent's refusal does today. Resuming a claim isn't a piece of work in this sense and runs beside anything.
2. The app does the first part of the work itself: it creates the dispatch's or the evaluation's run, creates the round, or records a handler's review of a proposal. It checks and writes in a short transaction of its own, committed before it asks the agent, and holds a lock while it checks, so two requests at the same moment can't both start. A request uses one database connection at a time. It then asks the agent to work the run or the round by its id, with three operations in place of the four that start work today: `{"operation": "work_run", "run_id": ...}` for a dispatch or an evaluation, `{"operation": "work_round", "round_id": ...}`, and `{"operation": "carry_round_on", "round_id": ..., "proposal_id": ...}` after a review. The agent no longer creates runs or rounds, apart from the evaluations a round makes as it goes, and no longer records reviews: when it carries a round on, it reads the proposal's review and reason from the app.
3. When the agent didn't take the work (step 3), the app undoes it: a run is marked stopped with the reason, a new round is stopped, and a review is cleared, so the proposal waits for a review again. The handler gets the answer they get today.
4. The agent no longer marks runs or rounds stopped when it gets ready. When its work on a run or a round fails, it stops only that run or round, by its id, and stopping a round stops its running evaluation. It refuses to work a run that isn't running, or a round that isn't open.
5. While the agent works on a run, a round or a resumed claim, it tells the app so every minute; a round's word covers the evaluation it's working on. Work the app hasn't heard about for five minutes counts as stopped, since a session ended or the agent on the laptop restarted, counting from the later of when the app started it (creating the run or the round, or recording the review) and the agent's last word. When a handler next asks to start work, the app marks such work stopped with the reason "The agent stopped answering", stopping a round with its evaluation, and the new work goes ahead. The app never marks a round's evaluation stopped on its own. The dashboard shows a run's or a round's reason for stopping.
6. The agent no longer keeps one piece of work at a time itself. On Runtime each session is a process of its own, so only the app's check can keep one at a time.

Done when:

- Tests cover a second start refused while a dispatch, an evaluation or a working round is going, two starts at the same moment, a start after work went quiet for five minutes, a start the agent didn't take, a timeout that leaves the work going, a review cleared when the agent didn't take it, a failed run that stops only itself, and a resume beside a dispatch.
- The end-to-end tests pass with the app creating runs, rounds and reviews.
- With `npm run dev` running, a dispatch at N=25 works the queue, and starting another while it runs is refused on the dashboard.

## Notes from our last build

These notes come from our own build of the context layer, on the `week3day3-end` branch, and from our team's experiments with AgentCore Runtime. If your build took a different path, a note may not match your code: follow its intent where it applies and skip it where it doesn't.

- **What our deployment expects:** `agentcore/agentcore.json` declares the runtime `agent`, built from `runtime/`, and the three credentials, named as the settings they become. `agentcore/cdk/lib/cdk-stack.ts` stages `runtime/` and `agent/` together at each deploy and lists the `runtime` group's packages (`uv export --only-group runtime`) for the build. `runtime/main.py` imports `app` from `agent.entrypoint` and runs it with `app.run()`, which serves it on port 8080, on 127.0.0.1 on a laptop. The deployment gives the agent `AGENTCORE_CREDENTIAL_<NAME>_NAME` for each credential. Our deployment notebook runs `agentcore deploy` with the app's address, the Gateway's URL and the Memory's id in `agentcore/.env.local`.
- **The AgentCore app** (`bedrock-agentcore` 1.24): `BedrockAgentCoreApp` is a Starlette app, so uvicorn serves it and Starlette's `TestClient` calls it. `@app.entrypoint` marks the function that takes each payload, with the request's context when the function has a second parameter named `context`, and a plain `def` runs in Starlette's thread pool. What it returns is the JSON answer, with status 200; an `HTTPException` from `starlette.exceptions` sets the status and answers `{"error": <detail>}`; any other exception answers a 500. `context.request_headers` carries the `Authorization` header, and is `None` when a request has no headers it passes on. `app.add_async_task(name)` returns an id and `app.complete_async_task(id)` ends that task, both safe from any thread, and its `/ping` says `HealthyBusy` while any task is registered. `requires_api_key(provider_name=...)` from `bedrock_agentcore.identity.auth` fetches a credential's value from Identity: it's a decorator that passes the value to the function it wraps as `api_key`, and it makes its client when it's applied, so apply it while getting ready. It reads the token Runtime sends with each request, so the agent fetches its credentials while it handles a request, never on a thread of its own. In our experiment on Runtime V2 with the one-minute idle timeout, a session whose work was registered as an async task kept working through two minutes with no requests, and the session ended after its work was done. A session whose work wasn't registered was ended, with its work, a minute after its last request.
- **Runtime sessions:** each session is a microVM of its own. On V2 every session starts from one snapshot, with the same process id, 1, and the same random numbers until something reseeds them. In our experiment an evaluation of the five tiny claims ran on Runtime through the Gateway, and a graph paused in one Runtime session resumed in another through `PostgresSaver` on the transaction pooler.
- **The pause:** on a resume LangGraph runs the interrupted node again from the top, so nothing goes before the `interrupt()` in its node. `Command(resume=...)` resumes a thread, with `durability="sync"` as now. `graph.get_state(config).interrupts` lists the interrupts waiting for an answer; `next` names the pause as soon as the step before it is saved, before the pause has run, while the claim already shows as waiting for sign-off.
- **The claim graph** is in `agent/harness.py`: `after_tools` sends an approval to `pay` or ends the work, `pay` holds a payment the Gateway refuses with `hold`, and the end of the work is `remember` when there's a memory. `claim_graphs(version, kind, pool)` knows the kind of run when it builds a run's graphs, as it does for memory. The graph's state keeps its own `decision`, `status` and `model_calls` (`agent/tools.py`); the 30-call limit counts across the whole thread. `carry_on` and `work_in_run` show how a claim's work is picked up again, and the dispatcher's `list_claims` chooses the claims a dispatch works (`agent/dispatcher.py`). A decision's version is only in the text of `decided_by`.
- **The app:** `record_decision`, `sign_off_claim` and `hold_payment` are in `backend/main.py`, the decisions table, with one row for each claim, in `backend/database.py`, and `SignsOffOverTheLimit` is the check on the handler. The sign-off panel is `SignOffForm.tsx` inside `DecisionPanel.tsx`, in `dashboard/src/components`. `backend/instructions/claim.md` says a decision "can't be changed afterwards", which a send back changes.
- **Transactions:** the `Database` dependency commits when the request ends (`backend/dependencies.py`), so anything the agent must see has to be committed in a transaction of its own before the app calls it. A check followed by an insert doesn't stop two starts at Postgres's default isolation, and SQLite through pysqlite only begins a transaction at its first write. A conditional update of one row, checking the row count, holds on both and through the transaction pooler; `finish_run` in `backend/runs.py` is the model.
- **The agent's service, as the app calls it:** `backend/agent_service.py` sends each request with `send(path, body)`, with a 10-second timeout. The app's routes that call it are in `backend/runs.py` and `backend/improvement.py`, where `refuse_while_a_round_is_open` already keeps one round open at a time and `approve_proposal` and `reject_proposal` pass reviews on for the service to record. The service's routes, `OneAtATime`, the lifespan's `api.stop_runs()` and `improver_api.stop_rounds(RESTARTED)`, and the failure paths' `api.stop_runs()` in `work_in_background` and `stop_rounds` in `stop_the_round` are in `agent/service.py`. Today the service creates runs and rounds through the app's `create_dispatch`, `create_evaluation` and `create_round`, by way of `agent/api.py` and `agent/improver_api.py`. Runs have no column for why they stopped. A round's evaluations are created by the service as the round goes (`agent/service.py`, `agent/improver.py`), `decide` in `backend/improvement.py` rolls back a version with a stopped evaluation, and `stop` in `backend/round_state.py` doesn't touch runs. `RoundForService` in `backend/models.py` carries no review. The backend's pool on the laptop is four connections with no overflow (`backend/database.py`). The service's tests are in `agent/tests/test_service.py`, through FastAPI's `TestClient`, with a check of its OpenAPI document against the API rules and a test that it has no resume operation; `agent/tests/conftest.py`, `test_connection.py` and `test_improver.py` import it too, and `test_boundaries.py` names it among the modules that never import a route.
- **Runtime from boto3:** `boto3.client("bedrock-agentcore", config=Config(retries={"total_max_attempts": 1}, connect_timeout=5, read_timeout=20)).invoke_agent_runtime(agentRuntimeArn=..., runtimeSessionId=..., payload=json.dumps(...))` returns the entrypoint's JSON as a stream in `response["response"]`. botocore's own `ConnectionError`, from `botocore.exceptions`, and its subclasses mean the request never got there, and a `ClientError` is an error answer from Runtime. Anything else raised once the request was sent, such as botocore's `HTTPClientError` (which includes `ReadTimeoutError`) or an `IncompleteReadError` while reading the answer, means it may have got there. Our deployment gives the app's role `bedrock-agentcore:InvokeAgentRuntime` on the runtime.
- **The transaction pooler:** the backend on Lambda already turns prepared statements off with a connection for each request (`backend/database.py`). In our experiment the agent's own pool with `prepare_threshold=None` passed 30 of 30 queries on port 6543.
- **The checks:** `check:server` and `check:context` in `package.json` are the model for the runtime group's check. Ruff and pyright don't cover `runtime/` today; add it to both, as the platform team's file passes them. The end-to-end tests share one database (`playwright.config.ts`), and the agent's stand-in (`e2e/agent_stand_in.ts`) answers the service's routes today, with Playwright waiting for its `/api/health`; it moves to `/invocations` and `/ping`, as the agent does. The stand-in starts nothing and gets only `PORT`, so once the app creates runs, the stand-in needs the app's address and the agent's key to finish or stop the work it's given, or every later start gets a 409.

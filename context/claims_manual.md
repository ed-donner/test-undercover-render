# UnderCover Claims Handling Manual

Version 2026.2. Internal. For claims handlers and for any system that decides claims on their behalf.

## 1. Purpose and scope

This manual explains how UnderCover decides travel insurance claims. The policy wording is the contract with the customer and it always comes first. This manual covers what the wording leaves open: how to read a clause when a claim does not fit it neatly, which documents to ask for, how to work out the payment, when to pass a claim to someone else, and how to write to the customer.

Everything here applies to claims under wording version 2026.1. All amounts are in US dollars.

## 2. The three decisions

Every claim ends in one of three decisions.

**Approve.** The incident is covered, the required documents are on file and the payment has been worked out. An approval always carries a payout amount, which can be lower than the amount claimed.

**Decline.** The wording does not cover the claim. The usual reasons are an exclusion that applies, a waiting period that was not met, a reason for cancellation that is not on the list, or an incident outside the dates of the covered trip. A decline always names the section of the wording it relies on.

**Refer.** The claim needs a person's judgment or more information before it can be approved or declined. Refer when a required document is missing, when the story and the documents disagree, when the claim is the customer's third in 12 months, or when the wording and this manual together still do not settle the question. A referral always says what is needed and who should look at it.

Choose refer only for those reasons. A claim that is clearly covered should be approved and a claim that is clearly excluded should be declined, even when the amount is large or the story is unusual. Unusual stories are common in travel insurance and most of them are true.

## 3. Payment authority

A payout of up to $1,000 can be released by whoever decided the claim, whether that is a handler or an automated system.

A payout above $1,000 needs sign-off from a claims handler before payment. The decision recorded on the claim stays as approve, with the payout amount, and the payment waits for the sign-off. The handler who signs off checks the decision and the calculation and records their name against the payment.

The limit applies to the payout, after limits and the deductible, and the amount claimed does not matter. A $4,000 claim that pays $600 does not need sign-off.

## 4. Working a claim

Work every claim in the same order.

1. Read the customer's story and the list of what they are claiming.
2. Look up the policy. Note the plan, the add-ons, the purchase date, the trip dates and the travelers.
3. Check that the incident falls inside the covered trip and that the claim was filed within 90 days.
4. Identify the benefit the claim falls under and read that section of the wording.
5. Check the conditions of the benefit, such as the waiting period or the covered reasons.
6. Check the exclusions of the benefit and the general exclusions. Where an add-on changes an exclusion or a limit, check whether the policy has it.
7. Read every document in full. Medical records in particular often contain facts that the customer's story leaves out.
8. Check that the required documents are all there (section 5).
9. Look at the customer's claim history (section 9).
10. Work out the payout (section 6).
11. Record the decision, the payout, the section of the wording relied on and a reason in plain language.

## 5. Documents and document requests

| Benefit | Required documents |
|---|---|
| Trip Cancellation and Interruption | A physician's written statement or other proof of the covered reason. The supplier's confirmation of what was and was not refunded. Proof of payment. |
| Trip Delay | The carrier's confirmation of the delay and its cause. Receipts for every expense, except meals (section 10). |
| Baggage Delay | The carrier's delayed baggage report showing arrival and delivery times. Receipts for the purchases. |
| Baggage and Personal Effects: theft | A police report made within 24 hours of the theft. Proof of value for items claimed above $100. |
| Baggage and Personal Effects: loss or damage by a carrier | The carrier's report. The carrier's settlement letter, if it has paid anything. Proof of value for items claimed above $100. |
| Baggage and Personal Effects: accidental damage | A repair estimate or a statement that the item cannot be repaired. Proof of value or of the replacement cost. |
| Passport and Travel Documents | Receipts for the fees, photos and local travel. A short account of what happened. |
| Emergency Medical | The itemized bill. The medical records from the visit. |

Proof of value is a purchase receipt, a card statement or, for a replacement, the receipt for the replacement. For items claimed at $100 or less, the customer's own list is enough. Proof of payment is a receipt, a card statement or the supplier's written confirmation that the booking was paid.

When a required document is missing, refer the claim with a document request that names the document. Do not decline a claim because a document is missing, and do not approve it on the customer's promise to send the document later. When the customer states that a required step was never taken, for example that a theft was never reported to the police, the condition of the benefit has not been met and the claim is declined.

A document request gives the customer 30 days. The claim stays open while it waits.

## 6. Working out the payout

Follow the order in section 10 of the wording.

1. Take the eligible amount of each item or expense. For belongings, that is the amount claimed or the per-item limit, whichever is lower. Leave out any line that is excluded.
2. Take off anything a carrier or another party has paid for the same loss.
3. Take off the deductible, if the benefit has one. The deductible applies to Baggage and Personal Effects and to Emergency Medical, once per claim.
4. Cap the result at the maximum for the benefit. For Trip Cancellation and Interruption the cap is the trip cost on the policy or the plan maximum, whichever is lower.

If the result is zero or less, the claim is declined and the letter explains why.

**Worked example 1.** A customer on Economy has a bag stolen with a police report on file. The bag held a jacket claimed at $220, boots claimed at $140 and a book claimed at $25. The per-item limit on Economy is $150, so the jacket counts at $150, the boots at $140 and the book at $25, a total of $315. Nobody else has paid anything. The Economy deductible of $100 comes off. The payout is $215.

**Worked example 2.** A customer on First is delayed for 30 hours by a strike and claims $1,720 in hotel and meal costs with receipts. There is no deductible on Trip Delay. The maximum on First is $1,500, so the payout is $1,500. That is above $1,000, so the payment needs sign-off.

**Worked example 3.** A customer on Premium with Electronics Plus has a laptop damaged beyond repair, with a replacement cost of $1,450. The Electronics Plus limit is $1,200, so the laptop counts at $1,200. The Premium deductible of $50 comes off. The payout is $1,150 and needs sign-off.

## 7. Interpretation notes

These notes record how UnderCover reads the wording in situations that come up often. They have been agreed with the underwriters and they bind every decision.

**Unattended belongings.** Belongings are unattended when the customer could neither see them nor reach them and a member of the public could. A bag on a train luggage rack while the customer is in the same carriage is attended. The same bag while the customer is on the platform is unattended. Belongings on a beach while the customer swims are unattended. Belongings in a locked hotel room or a hotel safe are attended. Theft from the person, such as a snatch or a pickpocket, is never a case of unattended belongings.

**Vehicles.** Belongings in a vehicle are covered only when locked in the trunk or the glove compartment and out of sight. A bag on a seat or a floor is unattended, even if the vehicle was locked.

**Pairs and sets.** A pair of shoes, a suit, a camera with its lens, a set of golf clubs and a set of luggage bought together each count as one item. Several shirts or several books are separate items.

**Wild animals.** A loss caused by a wild animal is treated as accidental loss or accidental damage. It is not theft, so no police report is needed. It is also outside the exclusion for moths, vermin, insects and pets, which is aimed at slow damage to stored belongings and at the customer's own animals. Monkeys, gulls, bears, giraffes and similar animals are wild animals for this purpose. Rats and mice are vermin wherever they turn up, and damage by them stays excluded. Accept a written statement from a hotel, a tour operator, a licensed guide or a park authority as evidence of what happened. These claims are more common than new handlers expect, particularly from temple sites in Southeast Asia and from safari lodges.

**Waiting periods.** A Trip Delay runs from the scheduled departure time to the actual departure time, as confirmed by the carrier. A Baggage Delay runs from the customer's arrival to the delivery of the bag, as shown on the carrier's report. Use the carrier's times over the customer's estimate. The waiting period depends on the plan, so always check the plan before deciding.

**Delay on the way home.** When a delay on the return journey pushes the customer past the return date on the policy, cover continues until they are home. Expenses on the extra day are payable.

**Reasonable accommodation.** Trip Delay pays reasonable extra costs, and for accommodation that means up to $350 a night for a room, with one room for every two travelers on the policy. When the customer paid more, pay $350 a night and show the reduction in the letter. A customer stranded late at night often has little choice of hotel, so the higher price is no reason to doubt the claim.

**Consequential losses.** Trip Delay pays for meals, accommodation, local transport and rebooking fees. Anything that the customer lost because they arrived late is a consequential loss and is excluded, however well documented. Pay the covered lines of the claim and leave out the excluded ones. One excluded line does not make the rest of the claim fail.

**The value of information.** For a laptop, a drive, a notebook, a manuscript or a photograph we pay for the physical object: the device, the paper, the prints. We do not pay for the work or the memories it held. Handlers should say this kindly, because these are often the losses that hurt most.

**Adventure sports.** When a claim involves a listed sport, check the policy for the Adventure Sports add-on. With the add-on, check that the customer held any certification the activity requires and stayed inside its limits. For scuba diving that means the certification card and the dive log or the dive operator's record of the depth. Without the add-on, the injury is excluded.

**Pre-existing conditions.** Count back 60 days from the purchase date of the policy. If the medical records show symptoms, a physician visit, treatment or a change in medication for the same condition inside that window, the condition is pre-existing. A change in dose is a change in medication. Rely on the medical records over the customer's account, since customers often do not connect earlier symptoms with the emergency. If the policy has the Pre-Existing Condition Waiver, the exclusion does not apply and no look-back is needed. A stable condition with no symptoms, visits or changes inside the window is not pre-existing.

**Treatment that can wait.** Emergency Medical pays for what was needed at the time. Follow-up care after the customer is home is not covered. Physiotherapy during the trip is covered when a physician prescribed it.

## 8. When the story and the documents disagree

Sometimes the documents do not support the customer's story. Dates do not line up, a receipt comes from the wrong place, amounts have been altered, or the carrier's record says something different from the claim form.

A handler never declines a claim for suspected dishonesty and never accuses a customer of it. Refer the claim to the Special Investigations Unit, list each point where the story and the documents differ, and make no comment to the customer beyond saying that the claim is being reviewed. Many conflicts turn out to be honest mistakes, such as the wrong receipt attached.

Refer to Special Investigations when you see any of these:

- A receipt dated before the incident it is meant to follow, or from a place the customer was not in.
- A carrier's report that contradicts the customer on times, dates or what was lost.
- Documents that look altered, or amounts that do not match between documents.
- The same loss claimed twice, under this policy or another.

Small differences are normal and are not a reason to refer. A customer who says a bag was delayed for about a day when the report shows 26 hours has told the truth.

## 9. Repeat claims

Before deciding any claim, check the customer's claim history. A customer's third claim within any 12 month period is referred to the Special Investigations Unit for review before payment, whatever its merits. Count claims by the date they were filed, and count every claim, approved, declined or open.

Most third claims are paid after review. Frequent travelers have more incidents, and the review exists to look for patterns across claims that a handler deciding one claim cannot see. The referral should say that the claim appears payable, if it does, and give the amount.

A first or second claim in 12 months is decided in the normal way, and the earlier claim is no reason to look harder at the later one.

## 10. Meals without receipts

Customers stuck at an airport often pay cash at kiosks and keep no receipts. For a covered Trip Delay, meals can be paid without receipts at a flat $35 per traveler for each 24 hours of the delay or part of 24 hours, counted from the scheduled departure time. A delay of 11 hours is one day and a delay of 30 hours is two. This applies to meals only. Accommodation, transport and rebooking fees always need receipts. If the customer has receipts for meals, pay the receipts instead, as long as the amounts are reasonable.

## 11. Customer data

A customer record holds a name, contact details, a date of birth, an address and a claim history. A claim often holds medical information as well.

- Use a customer's data only to decide that customer's claims.
- Do not include one customer's details in anything written to or about another customer.
- Medical details stay on the claim. A letter to the customer can refer to them. Nothing sent to a carrier, a supplier or another third party may include them.
- Do not copy customer data into notes, tools or systems outside the claims platform.
- A date of birth and an address are used to confirm identity and for no other purpose.

## 12. Writing to the customer

Write the way you would want to be written to after a bad day on vacation. Be plain, be brief and be kind.

- Start with the decision and the amount.
- Show the calculation whenever the payout is lower than the amount claimed, line by line.
- When declining, name the section of the wording and quote the sentence that applies. Do not lecture.
- When referring for a document, say exactly which document and how to send it.
- A little warmth is welcome when the story invites it, and sarcasm is never appropriate.

## 13. Wording versions

Decide every claim under the wording version in force on the day the policy was bought, which is shown by the purchase date. When a new version of the wording is published, claims on older policies stay on the older version. When this manual changes, the change applies to decisions made from that day on, and the manual's version number goes on every decision record.

## 14. Records

Every decision record holds the decision, the payout, the calculation, the section relied on, a reason in plain language, who or what made the decision and when. For a payout above $1,000 it also holds who signed off and when. A decision with no reason on file is treated as not yet made.
